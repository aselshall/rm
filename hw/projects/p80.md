## Project 2: Quantifying Nitrogen and Phosphorus Leaching from Sandy Agricultural Soils Using Field Lysimeters

### Supervisors

Ahmed S. Elshall, Ph.D.    
U.A. Whitaker College of Engineering and The Water School    
Florida Gulf Coast University   

### Background and Motivation

Southwest Florida’s sandy soils have low water- and nutrient-holding capacity. Rainfall and irrigation can rapidly move applied fertilizers below the crop root zone, reducing fertilizer-use efficiency and transporting nitrogen and phosphorus toward groundwater and downstream surface waters.

Humic acid may improve nutrient and water retention by increasing the ability of sandy soil to retain applied nutrients within the root zone. The larger EPA-funded project will evaluate whether humic acid can allow substantial reductions in nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizer application while maintaining or improving tomato production. The project connects agricultural management in the Immokalee area to nutrient loading in the Peace River–Charlotte Harbor system and the risk of harmful algal blooms. 

This student project will focus on the controlled lysimeter experiment at the University of Florida/IFAS Southwest Florida Research and Education Center in Immokalee. Suction plate-type field lysimeters will be used to collect drainage below the tomato root zone. Because the lysimeters remain under field conditions, the collected leachate can be used to quantify deep drainage and nutrient losses caused by rainfall and irrigation. 

The project will integrate water-volume measurements, water-quality analyses, soil-moisture monitoring, weather observations, fertilizer records, and crop measurements to develop complete water and nutrient balances for the experimental treatments.

### Project Objective

The objective is to quantify total nitrogen and total phosphorus leaching from sandy agricultural soil and determine whether humic acid can reduce nutrient losses when nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizer application is reduced.

### Key Research Questions

1. How much water drains below the tomato root zone following rainfall and irrigation events?  
2. What are the concentrations and mass loads of total nitrogen and total phosphorus in lysimeter drainage? 
3. How do TN and TP leaching losses differ among standard fertilizer, humic-acid treatment, and zero-fertilizer conditions?  
4. Can humic acid reduce fertilizer application while maintaining or improving tomato growth, yield, and nutrient uptake?   
5. Does humic acid alter soil-moisture storage and the timing or magnitude of drainage events?  
6. Which nitrogen and phosphorus forms contribute most to the total nutrient loads leaving the root zone?   
7. How do rainfall, irrigation, antecedent soil moisture, and crop development affect nutrient-leaching events?  
8. Do nutrient losses vary between growing seasons?  

### Experimental Design

The experiment will be conducted using suction plate-type field lysimeters at the UF/IFAS SWFREC site in Immokalee.

The larger study proposes a complete block design with three general treatment groups:

1. **Standard-practice control:** Tomatoes receive the standard science-based nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizer application rate.

2. **Humic-acid treatment:** Humic acid is applied with reduced fertilizer inputs. The proposal identifies fertilizer-reduction levels of 25% and 50%.

3. **Zero-fertilizer control:** No fertilizer is applied. This treatment is used to estimate background nutrient mineralization and leaching from the soil.

The experiment will extend across two tomato growing seasons to evaluate treatment performance under different environmental conditions. 

The student will help refine the experimental layout, replication structure, sampling schedule, event definitions, and quality-assurance procedures during EGN 6457.

### Data Collection

#### 1. Lysimeter Drainage

Leachate draining below the root zone will be collected following irrigation and rainfall events.

For each collection event, students will record:

* Date and time of collection.
* Lysimeter and treatment identification.
* Drainage volume.
* Time since the preceding rainfall or irrigation event.
* Rainfall and irrigation amounts.
* Sample condition and any field observations.
* Plant-growth stage and recent fertilizer or humic-acid applications.

Measured drainage volume will be used to calculate deep drainage and nutrient mass loss from each lysimeter.

#### 2. Water-Quality Analysis

Leachate samples will be analyzed for:

* Total nitrogen.
* Total phosphorus.
* Nitrate.
* Ammonium or ammonia.
* Soluble reactive phosphorus.
* Other nutrient forms included in the approved analytical plan.

The larger project specifically identifies TN, TP, nitrate, ammonia, and soluble reactive phosphorus as target constituents for quantifying nutrient movement below the root zone. 

Field and laboratory quality-control procedures may include duplicates, blanks, calibration checks, sample-preservation records, chain-of-custody documentation, and detection-limit review.

#### 3. Soil-Moisture Monitoring

In-situ soil-moisture sensors will continuously measure changes in water content within the soil profile.

The monitoring system may include:

* METER TEROS soil-moisture sensors.
* METER ZL6 data loggers.
* ZENTRA Cloud data storage.
* Sensors installed at multiple depths within or adjacent to the root zone.

The measurements will be used to determine:

* Soil wetting following rainfall and irrigation.
* Soil-water depletion between events.
* Differences in water retention among treatments.
* Soil-moisture conditions preceding major drainage events.

The Surface Water-Groundwater Field Lab includes TEROS soil-moisture sensors, ZL6 loggers, and related equipment for near-real-time water-budget measurements. 

#### 4. Weather and Irrigation Data

A METER ATMOS 41W weather station will provide meteorological observations, including precipitation and other variables needed to interpret water losses and crop-water demand. The lysimeter study will also require detailed irrigation records. 

Relevant variables may include:

* Rainfall.
* Air temperature.
* Relative humidity.
* Wind speed and direction.
* Solar radiation.
* Atmospheric pressure.
* Irrigation timing and volume.

Weather, irrigation, soil moisture, and drainage will be integrated to develop a water balance for each treatment.

#### 5. Soil Characterization

Soil samples will be collected to characterize the conditions controlling nutrient retention and leaching.

Potential analyses include:

* Soil particle-size distribution.
* Soil texture.
* Initial and final nutrient concentrations.
* Organic matter.
* Soil pH.
* Cation-exchange capacity.
* Gravimetric water content.
* Bulk density, when available.

These measurements will help explain whether treatment effects are associated with changes in soil nutrient retention, organic matter, or water-storage properties.

The broader project proposes using crop biomass, yield, quality, shelf life, and plant-tissue nutrient content to assess nutrient uptake and treatment performance. 

### Data Analysis Approach

#### Water Balance

A water balance will be calculated for each lysimeter or treatment:

$$
\Delta S = P + I - D - ET
$$

where:

* $\Delta S$ is the change in soil-water storage,
* P is rainfall,
* I is irrigation,
* D is measured lysimeter drainage, and
* ET is evapotranspiration or the remaining water-balance component.

The analysis will evaluate how humic-acid treatment affects soil-water storage, drainage volume, and drainage timing.

#### Nutrient Concentration and Load

Nutrient mass lost during each event will be calculated as:

$$
M_{i,j}=C_{i,j}V_{i,j}
$$

where:

* $M_{i,j}$ is the nutrient mass lost from lysimeter (i) during event (j),
* $C_{i,j}$ is the measured nutrient concentration, and
* $V_{i,j}$ is the drainage volume.

Cumulative nutrient loss will be calculated by summing event loads across the growing season.

Nutrient losses may be expressed as:

* Mass per lysimeter.
* Mass per unit area.
* Percentage of applied fertilizer.
* Load per unit crop yield.
* Flow-weighted mean concentration.

#### Treatment Comparisons

The analysis will compare:

* Standard-practice control versus humic-acid treatment.
* Standard-practice control versus zero-fertilizer control.
* Different fertilizer-reduction levels.
* Individual drainage events.
* Early-, middle-, and late-season periods.
* Results between growing seasons.

A statistical model appropriate for the block design and repeated observations will be used. Treatment, season, and time may be included as explanatory factors, with block or lysimeter included as a random effect when appropriate.

#### Event-Based Analysis

Individual rainfall and irrigation events will be examined to determine how nutrient losses depend on:

* Rainfall or irrigation amount.
* Event intensity.
* Time since fertilizer application.
* Antecedent soil moisture.
* Drainage volume.
* Crop-growth stage.
* Time elapsed since humic-acid application.

This analysis may identify conditions under which nutrient-leaching risk is greatest.

#### Nutrient-Use Efficiency

Crop uptake and leaching measurements will be combined to evaluate nutrient-use efficiency.

Potential indicators include:

$$
\text{Leaching fraction}=
\frac{\text{nutrient mass leached}}
{\text{nutrient mass applied}}
$$

and

$$
\text{Agronomic efficiency}=
\frac{\text{crop yield}}
{\text{nutrient mass applied}}
$$

The analysis will determine whether humic acid reduces nutrient losses while increasing the fraction of applied nutrients recovered by the crop.

### Why It Is Important

Nutrient concentrations alone do not fully describe agricultural pollution. A high concentration in a small drainage volume may produce less nutrient loss than a moderate concentration in a large drainage volume. Lysimeters allow both drainage volume and nutrient concentration to be measured, making it possible to calculate actual TN and TP mass loads.

This study will:

* Directly quantify nitrogen and phosphorus losses below the tomato root zone.
* Test whether humic acid reduces nutrient leaching from sandy soil.
* Determine whether fertilizer inputs can be reduced without reducing crop production.
* Identify the rainfall and irrigation conditions that generate the largest nutrient losses.
* Provide parameters for the subsequent field-scale experiment.
* Support watershed-scale estimates of nutrient-load reduction.
* Improve understanding of the connection between agricultural fertilizer management, groundwater quality, downstream nutrient loading, and harmful algal blooms.

The lysimeter experiment is the foundational component of the larger humic-acid project because it will provide controlled measurements needed for field validation and watershed modeling.
