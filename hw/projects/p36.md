### Background and Motivation

Urban development replaces natural and vegetated surfaces with buildings, roads, parking areas, and other impervious surfaces. These changes increase stormwater runoff while reducing opportunities for infiltration and temporary surface storage. Nature-based infrastructure (NBI), implemented through stormwater best management practices (BMPs), can help manage runoff by providing storage, infiltration, interception, and other hydrologic functions. However, the potential benefit of a BMP depends strongly on where it can physically be implemented and where implementation has the greatest relative hydrologic priority.

A recently developed geospatial framework addresses this problem by explicitly separating two questions: Where can a particular BMP be implemented? and, among those feasible locations, where should implementation receive greater priority? The framework combines high-resolution land-cover mapping, terrain and hydrologic characterization, rule-based BMP feasibility screening, event-based runoff and storage estimation, Analytic Hierarchy Process-based multicriteria decision analysis (AHP-MCDA), and sensitivity analysis.  The rule-based component identifies physically feasible locations, whereas the MCDA component differentiates relative hydrologic priority within those feasible areas. 

The framework was originally demonstrated in Bonita Bay, Southwest Florida, where sixteen BMP–runoff-source combinations associated with roofs, parking/roads, and pervious areas were evaluated.  Applying the framework to Naples, Florida provides an opportunity to evaluate its transferability to a different developed coastal setting and to determine how differences in land cover, development patterns, drainage, soils, groundwater conditions, and available open space affect BMP feasibility and prioritization.

### Project Objective

The objective of this project is to apply and evaluate a geospatial multicriteria framework for identifying feasible locations and prioritizing nature-based stormwater BMPs in Naples, Florida.

The study will determine how runoff-source characteristics and local physical and hydrologic conditions control BMP implementation opportunities and will evaluate whether the framework and siting criteria developed for another Southwest Florida community can be transferred to Naples or require modification.

### Key Research Questions

1. What are the spatial distributions of impervious surfaces, pervious areas, drainage features, soils, groundwater conditions, wetlands, flood-prone areas, and other features relevant to NBI planning in the Naples study area?

2. Where are individual stormwater BMPs physically feasible based on BMP-specific siting requirements?

3. Among physically feasible locations, which areas have the greatest relative hydrologic priority based on runoff generation, infiltration conditions, storage adequacy, drainage proximity, and terrain suitability?

4. How sensitive are the resulting priority maps to assumptions concerning MCDA criterion weights?

### Geospatial Analysis Approach

#### 1. Characterize Existing Hydrologic and Land-Cover Conditions

Students will develop the spatial layers required to characterize the existing study area, including impervious and pervious surfaces, buildings, roads and parking areas, vegetation, water bodies, drainage pathways, soils, groundwater conditions, and topography.

High-resolution imagery will be used to distinguish runoff-source categories at a spatial scale appropriate for individual BMP siting. Where necessary, students will evaluate alternative classification approaches and validate the resulting land-cover map.

#### 2. Identify BMP-Specific Feasible Areas

Students will implement rule-based spatial screening to identify locations that satisfy minimum siting requirements for individual BMPs.

Candidate BMPs may include:

Roof runoff

* green roofs;
* rainwater harvesting;
* bioswales;
* bioretention/rain gardens; and
* trees.

Parking/road runoff

* pervious pavement;
* infiltration trenches;
* bioswales;
* bioretention/rain gardens; and
* trees.

Pervious-area runoff

* bioswales;
* infiltration basins;
* constructed wetlands;
* dry ponds;
* wet ponds; and
* trees.

The original framework evaluates these practices using combinations of runoff source, location requirements, proximity to impervious surfaces, slope, and groundwater-depth constraints.  Students will critically evaluate these criteria rather than automatically adopting them. Any Naples-specific modifications must be documented and justified through literature, regulatory guidance, physical reasoning, or data limitations.

Each BMP analysis will produce a binary feasibility layer identifying locations that meet all required screening criteria.

#### 3. Estimate Stormwater Runoff and BMP Storage Potential

Students will estimate spatially distributed event runoff using the Curve Number method for a selected design storm.

Runoff generation will be evaluated according to land cover and hydrologic soil conditions. BMP storage will then be estimated within each BMP-specific feasible area using appropriate screening-level storage assumptions.

The analysis must clearly distinguish potential storage-based runoff retention from simulated BMP performance. The original framework does not perform detailed runoff routing from individual source areas to BMPs, and its outputs represent screening-level potential rather than predicted flood reduction. 

Students should explicitly state these assumptions and limitations when interpreting their results.

#### 4. Prioritize Feasible BMP Locations Using AHP-MCDA

After feasibility screening, students will rank only the feasible locations using an AHP-MCDA framework.

Candidate prioritization criteria include:

* runoff-generation potential;
* saturated hydraulic conductivity;
* storage adequacy;
* drainage proximity; and
* slope or terrain suitability.

The original framework gives greatest weight to runoff generation and saturated hydraulic conductivity, followed by storage adequacy, drainage proximity, and slope.  Students will evaluate whether these criteria and their relative weights remain appropriate for Naples.

The distinction between feasibility and prioritization must remain explicit:

Feasibility screening asks:
*Can this BMP reasonably be implemented here?*

Prioritization asks:
*Among feasible locations, where should this BMP receive greater hydrologic priority?*

#### 5. Evaluate Criterion Redundancy and Sensitivity

Students will evaluate relationships among prioritization criteria using correlation analysis and, where appropriate, Principal Component Analysis.

A sensitivity analysis will then examine how changes in criterion weights affect:

* priority scores;
* priority-class areas; and
* spatial agreement among priority maps.

This analysis will help determine whether priority locations are robust or strongly dependent on subjective weighting assumptions.

#### 6. Evaluate Framework Transferability

A final analysis will compare the Naples application with findings from the Bonita Bay application.

The comparison should focus on process and spatial patterns, rather than expecting Naples to reproduce Bonita Bay results. Students will evaluate how differences in:

* impervious-surface configuration;
* building density;
* roads and parking;
* available pervious land;
* groundwater depth;
* soils;
* drainage networks; and
* hydrologically sensitive areas

influence feasible BMP opportunities and relative priority.

This is potentially the most scientifically interesting part of the student project.

### Why It Is Important

Stormwater BMP planning cannot be based solely on identifying undeveloped or pervious land. A location may be physically capable of accommodating a BMP yet provide relatively limited hydrologic benefit, while another feasible location may intercept runoff from a major impervious source or provide more favorable infiltration and storage conditions.

The project therefore introduces students to an important distinction between engineering feasibility and hydrologic prioritization. It also provides experience integrating remote sensing, GIS, hydrology, groundwater conditions, engineering judgment, and multicriteria decision analysis within a single research problem.

From a research perspective, the Naples application will test whether a framework developed and demonstrated in one Southwest Florida community can be transferred to another. The study can therefore identify both the generalizable components of the methodology and the site-specific assumptions that require modification.

The priority maps should be interpreted as screening-level decision-support products, not final BMP designs or predictions of actual flood reduction. The manuscript likewise emphasizes that its outputs represent relative suitability and runoff-retention potential and depend on runoff estimation, assumed storage depths, AHP weighting, and siting rules.
